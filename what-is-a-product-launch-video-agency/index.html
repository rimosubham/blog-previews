What Is a Product Launch Video Agency? (Film, Distribution, or Both)

What a launch video agency actually does, the three types you'll meet (film-only, film plus distribution, amplification-only), what should be in scope, and how to pick the right one for your launch.

Subah Wadhwani · Co-founder & CEO, launch video and growth marketingUpdated 8 Oct 2026 · 18 min read · Checked against 6 sources
Featured image: the three types of product launch video agency (placeholder)

In short: A product launch video agency is a specialist that makes the short "hero" video a company posts to announce a product, feature or funding round, and often also runs the launch-day distribution that gets it seen. There are three types. Film-only studios script and produce the video and hand it over. Film-plus-distribution agencies also coordinate creators, timing and amplification on X and LinkedIn. Amplification-only services push a video you already made. Published prices run from about $3,500 to $25,000+, and published timelines from 5 business days to 6 weeks.

Disclosure: this guide is published by a launch video agency that offers film plus distribution and amplification only, one of the types described here. As a matter of editorial policy we don't name or link to any agencies in our guides, including our own. Instead we describe each type of provider and the prices that type publishes. Every price for other providers comes from a provider's own website as of 8 October 2026. Where a type of agency usually doesn't publish prices, we say so instead of guessing.

What is a product launch video agency?

A product launch video agency is a creative partner that plans, scripts and produces the video at the centre of an announcement: a new product, a major feature, a funding round, or a company coming out of stealth. The best ones also help decide how the video is released: on which day, from which account, with which accounts reposting it in the first hour, and what happens with the attention once it arrives.

That second half is what makes it a different business from a general video production company. A production company is hired to deliver a polished asset. A launch video agency is hired for a moment. Its work is measured in the first 24–48 hours after posting: views from people who weren't following you, replies and quote posts, homepage traffic, signups, demo requests or pre-orders.

The category has grown because launch behaviour has changed. For most venture-backed tech startups, "launching" now means a founder post on X and LinkedIn with a video attached, not a press release. On those feeds, the first hour decides how far the algorithm pushes a post. A strong video with no plan for that hour usually reaches the people who already follow you, and stops. As YC partner Kat Manalac puts it in her talk How to launch (again and again), "if you're like most startups, you will launch something, no one will care." Launch video agencies exist to change that outcome.

Video itself is no longer optional. In Wyzowl's 2026 survey, 91% of businesses use video as a marketing tool. Most make it in-house (59%), 32% mix in-house work with outside vendors, and 10% rely only on vendors. A launch is the moment many startups bring in outside help, because it's a one-shot asset with a fixed date.

What a launch video agency is not

  • Not an explainer studio by default. An explainer teaches how a product works and can run for two minutes or more. A launch video has to stop a scrolling stranger in under three seconds. Some studios do both, but they're different briefs.
  • Not a PR firm. PR agencies pitch journalists for coverage. Some launch agencies coordinate with your PR timing, but most don't pitch press themselves.
  • Not a view vendor. Buying bot views or engagement pods isn't launch distribution, and it can break platform rules. The legitimate version uses real, relevant accounts, with paid relationships disclosed.

1. Film-only launch video studios

A film-only studio scripts, shoots or animates, and edits the video, then hands it over. Many are excellent at craft. What they don't do is run the post itself. In practice you'll meet four sub-types:

  • Fixed-package launch studios. Built around accelerator launch weeks, these publish clear tiers. A typical published ladder is $5k (you shoot it, or it's fully animated), $8k (they film you in one city) and $12k (a small crew across several locations), with delivery in 5–9 business days. Some offer a refund if you're unhappy at the styleframe stage. Launch-day distribution usually isn't listed.
  • SaaS-focused launch studios. These typically offer founder-led, motion-graphics and commercial formats, with a discovery sprint, several script variants, and help writing the launch post and picking timing. Most quote after a discovery call rather than publishing a price.
  • Explainer and animation studios. These sit closer to the explainer end. Entry pricing is often published; one SaaS-focused studio says its premium projects start at $3,500.
  • Premium brand-film studios. High-craft cinematic or comedic films for well-funded companies, with unpublished prices and longer lead times.

Film-only is the right call when you already have distribution (a founder with a large, engaged following, a strong community, a big customer list) or when the video's main job is to live on your homepage and in sales emails for the next year.

2. Film-plus-distribution agencies

These agencies sell the video and the launch together. They build the asset for the launch mechanics (the hook, the first frame, the post copy), then coordinate who posts, when, and in what order.

  • Network-led launch agencies (our own model) produce founder-led launch videos end to end, then run launch-day distribution through a curated network of tech, founder, operator and investor accounts on X and LinkedIn. Some sell guaranteed 1M, 3M or 5M view tiers, quoted on a call.
  • Fixed-price studios with distribution tiers publish bundles such as $15,000 for an on-camera launch film plus distribution with a 100,000-view minimum, and $25,000 for the same film with a 500,000-view minimum. The same studios often sell a cheaper animated tier (around $5,000) with no distribution.
  • Outcome-priced, distribution-led agencies sell done-for-you launches with guaranteed multi-million view tiers (1.2M, 3M or 5M is one published ladder), a make-good if a launch misses, and pricing by application.
  • Full-crew production houses build the launch video (from about $25,000 and 4–6 weeks is a typical published floor) and can pair it with an X amplification service quoted separately.
  • Campaign-style GTM agencies package a founder-led film, an X thread, LinkedIn cuts and an influencer layer as one repeatable launch campaign.

This model is the right call when the launch has a hard business goal attached (a raise, a waitlist target, pre-orders, Demo Day) and you don't have the audience to hit it alone.

3. Amplification-only services

Amplification-only means someone else made the video, often the founding team, and the agency runs the distribution side. That usually covers building a list of relevant accounts, writing or reviewing their copy, setting the order and timing, and monitoring the first hours.

  • Launch agencies that also sell amplification only. We offer this too. Two of the launches credited on our homepage ran this way: an AI-for-science founder's self-made video (6.9M views on X) and a $500 brain-stimulation headset (2.5M). The founders made their videos, and we ran distribution.
  • Managed X amplification services often publish a typical CPM, for example $30–$50 per thousand views generated, with a make-good clause on the agreed reach.
  • Creator-repost marketplaces sell individual quote-reposts from vetted creators, priced per post, sometimes with an impression threshold below which you don't pay.

Pricing models differ (CPM, view tiers, flat campaign fees), so compare them on what's guaranteed, how views are counted, and what happens if the number is missed.

What should be in scope beyond editing the film?

This is the question founders ask most often, and it's where agency proposals differ the most. A full launch engagement usually covers seven jobs. Use this table to check which ones a proposal actually includes.

Job What it involves Film-only Film + distribution Amplification-only
1. Angle and positioning Finding the one sharp claim; deciding if this is a product, feature or funding story Usually Yes Sometimes (post copy only)
2. Script and hook First line and first 3 seconds; structure; one call to action Yes Yes No
3. Production Founder recording, B-roll, screen capture, motion design, sound Yes Yes No
4. Cutdowns and specs Hero cut, vertical and square versions, 6–15s cuts, captions, thumbnails Usually Yes No
5. Influencer and creator prep Building a list of relevant accounts, briefing them, drafting copy No Yes Yes
6. Launch-day coordination Picking the day and hour, sequencing posts, the first-hour "war room", replies Advice at most Yes Yes
7. Reporting and follow-through Views, engagement, traffic, signups; re-cuts for the next launch No Usually Usually

On top of those seven jobs, check three contract items that are easy to forget:

  • Rights and files. Who owns the footage and the final video? Under U.S. law, a commissioned video only counts as a "work made for hire" in certain categories and with a signed written agreement, so ask for a written assignment of copyright plus delivery of raw footage and project files.
  • Disclosure. If creators are paid or given something of value to post, U.S. rules expect the relationship to be disclosed. The FTC's Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers says a disclosure should be placed with the endorsement itself and be hard to miss. Ask the agency how its network handles this.

Launch video vs explainer video vs demo video

Agencies often use these terms loosely. They're different formats with different jobs, and one video rarely does all three well.

Launch video Explainer video Demo video
Job Announce that something new exists and reach people who weren't looking Teach the idea or category behind the product Show the product doing the work for someone already evaluating
Typical length 30–90 seconds 60–120+ seconds 2–5 minutes
Where it lives X, LinkedIn, launch posts, launch directories Homepage, onboarding, ads Website, sales follow-ups, pricing page
Measured by Retention, reach beyond your followers, launch-day actions Understanding, fewer support questions Conversion, sales-cycle speed
Lifespan Peaks in 24–48 hours, then re-cut Months to years Until the product changes

A practical point that launch specialists commonly make: script the launch video purely for retention, but capture extra product coverage during the same shoot so a demo can be cut later. Most launch videos made for X and LinkedIn land in the 60–90 second range, with a hook in the first three seconds.

How a launch video engagement works, step by step

Timelines vary, but a full film-plus-distribution engagement usually follows this shape. Work it back from your launch date.

  1. Intro call and fit (about 3–4 weeks out). Agree the goal (for example "1,000 waitlist signups in 48 hours" or "50 demo requests"), the launch date, and whether this is full production or amplification only.
  2. Angle and script (weeks 3–2). The agency proposes concepts and hooks. You approve one script. This is where most timelines slip, so block founder time now.
  3. Production (weeks 2–1). A shoot day for founder-led videos, or animation for motion-led ones. Screen recordings and extra takes get captured for later cuts.
  4. Edit and cutdowns (final week). Hero cut, platform versions, captions, thumbnail, and the post copy.
  5. Distribution prep (final week). Building and briefing the list of accounts that will repost, quote or reply. Agreeing the order and timing. Lining up any PR or investor posts.
  6. Launch day. The founder posts. The network engages in a planned order during the first hour or two. The team replies to comments, watches the numbers, and adjusts.
  7. After launch (days 1–7). Reporting, plus re-cuts for LinkedIn, ads or the next launch.

Faster is possible. One of our launches, for a seed-funded context-infrastructure startup that had no video or campaign assets, went from first call to launch in five days and reached 2M views in under two hours (3.7M total), with hundreds of demo requests. That was a compressed exception, not the norm. Published timelines elsewhere range from 5–9 business days at fixed-package launch studios and about 10 days for an animated launch video, to 4–6 weeks from kickoff at full-crew production houses. Agencies focused on brand launches may want to be involved 6–8 weeks before launch day.

The first hour on launch day, in practice

Because the first hour does so much of the work, ask any agency to describe its plan for it in writing. A good plan looks like this: the post, video, captions and thumbnail are approved before posting; the most relevant accounts reply and quote-post with their own take in the first 15 minutes; further waves follow in a planned order through the first hour, while the founder answers replies; and over the next day the team posts follow-ups (a thread, a demo clip, a LinkedIn version) and routes inbound leads to sales. If an agency can't describe this for your launch, it isn't really selling distribution.

Do you actually need a launch video agency?

Not always. Here's an honest way to decide.

You probably need a film-plus-distribution agency if:

  • the launch is tied to a raise, a major release, Demo Day or a pre-order window, and one strong day changes your quarter;
  • your company and founder accounts have small followings, so your own post will mostly reach people who already know you;
  • you don't have a marketer who can run a creator list and a launch-day war room;
  • the product isn't naturally viral (healthtech, infrastructure, enterprise) and needs a sharp angle to travel. A $500 brain-stimulation headset we launched is a good example: the launch drove six figures of pre-orders.

A film-only studio is probably enough if:

  • your founder already has a large, engaged audience on X or LinkedIn;
  • you have a strong community, customer list or partner network to activate;
  • the video's main job is evergreen: homepage, sales deck, onboarding.

You may not need an agency at all if:

  • it's a small feature update. A founder-shot video with a good first line, posted at a sensible time, is often enough;
  • you're pre-product. YC's advice is to launch early and often, to many small audiences, before spending on a big moment. Kat Manalac also warns early founders against pay-to-play blogger and influencer deals.

How to evaluate a launch video agency

Ask every agency on your shortlist the same questions, in writing.

  1. "Which type are you: film-only, film plus distribution, or amplification?" If they offer distribution, ask what exactly is included and what costs extra.
  2. "Can I see links to 3–5 live launch posts you ran?" Look at the replies and quote posts, not just the view count. Check the accounts that engaged. Are they relevant to your buyers?
  3. "Who will engage on launch day, and how are they chosen?" A curated list of founders, operators and investors in your space is worth more than a large list of unrelated accounts.
  4. "How are views counted, and what's guaranteed?" If a number is guaranteed, ask which platform metric it refers to, over what time window, and what happens if it's missed (re-run, make-good, refund). Our companion piece on whether guaranteed views on X are legit goes deeper on this.
  5. "How do paid creators disclose the relationship?" Real accounts with clear disclosure protect your brand. Bot views and undisclosed paid posts don't.
  6. "What's the timeline worked back from my launch date?" If they need 4–6 weeks and you launch in 10 days, they're the wrong fit, however good they are.
  7. "What do I own at the end?" Final files, raw footage, project files and the rights, in writing.
  8. "What happened on a launch that underperformed?" Every agency has one. How they answer tells you a lot.

Red flags

  • A view guarantee with no definition of a "view" and no make-good terms.
  • Showreels instead of links to live posts.
  • Engagement that doesn't match the views: millions of views with very few replies or quote posts is worth a follow-up question. Some distribution-led agencies publish a views-per-like check for this; whatever method you use, apply it to every agency, including us.
  • No clear owner for launch day ("you just post it").

The bottom line

A product launch video agency is defined less by the video it makes than by the launch it's accountable for. Decide first which of the three types you need: film-only if you already have distribution, film plus distribution if launch-day reach is the goal, amplification-only if the video is already made. Then compare prices, timelines and proof within that type.

Frequently asked questions

What is a product launch video agency?+

A product launch video agency plans, scripts and produces the short video a company posts to announce a product, feature or funding round. Many also run launch-day distribution on X and LinkedIn, coordinating which accounts repost and engage in the first hours so the video reaches people beyond your followers.

What does a launch video agency do beyond editing the film?+

A full-scope agency also finds the launch angle, writes the hook, delivers platform cutdowns, builds and briefs a list of relevant creator and founder accounts, sets launch-day timing and order, runs the first-hour response, and reports on views, traffic and signups afterwards.

What's the difference between a launch video agency and a video production company?+

A video production company delivers a finished asset. A launch video agency is built around a launch date and is judged on what happens after posting: reach, engagement and conversions. Many launch agencies also run distribution, which production companies usually don't.

What is amplification-only?+

Amplification-only means you or another studio made the video and the agency runs the distribution side: building the account list, timing and sequencing posts, and monitoring launch day. Some launch agencies offer it alongside full production, as do managed X amplification services and creator-repost marketplaces.

Is a launch video the same as an explainer video?+

No. A launch video announces something new and is built to stop a scrolling viewer, usually in 30–90 seconds. An explainer teaches how a product or category works and often runs longer. A demo video shows the product in detail for someone already evaluating it.

How much does a product launch video agency cost?+

Published prices run from about $3,500 at entry explainer-style studios and $5,000 at fixed-package launch studios to $25,000+ for full-crew productions or filmed launches bundled with a 500,000-view minimum. Agencies that guarantee multi-million view tiers usually quote on a call.

How long does a launch video agency take?+

Published timelines range from 5–9 business days at fixed-package launch studios and about 10 days for animation to 4–6 weeks at full-crew production houses. End-to-end production with distribution typically takes 2–3 weeks. Compressed five-day launches are possible but rare.

Do launch video agencies guarantee views?+

Some do. Fixed-price studios with distribution tiers publish 100,000- and 500,000-view minimums. Outcome-priced and network-led agencies guarantee multi-million tiers (1M, 3M and 5M is a common ladder), sometimes with a make-good. Film-only studios don't, because they don't run distribution.

How do I know if an agency's views are real?+

Ask for links to live posts, not screenshots. Check whether replies, quotes and likes are in proportion to the views, and whether the engaging accounts are relevant to your market. Ask how views are counted and what happens if a guarantee is missed.

Should paid creators disclose that they're promoting my launch?+

If a creator is paid or gets something of value for posting, U.S. FTC guidance says the relationship should be disclosed clearly, placed with the post itself. Ask your agency how its network handles disclosure before you sign.

Is a launch video agency worth it for a seed-stage startup?+

It's usually worth it when the launch is tied to a raise, Demo Day, a waitlist goal or pre-orders and your own audience is small. For small feature updates, a founder-shot video with a strong first line is often enough.

Do I need a launch video agency and a separate X amplification agency?+

Not necessarily. A film-plus-distribution agency covers both. If you already have a video, an amplification-only service may be enough. Using two vendors works too, but someone has to own launch day end to end.

Who owns the launch video when the project ends?+

Whoever your contract says. Ask for a written copyright assignment, or a work-made-for-hire clause where it applies, plus delivery of the final files, raw footage and project files.
